# On keeping a notebook nobody will read

I have kept a notebook for eleven years. It is not a diary and it is certainly not a system. It is a place where I put sentences before I know what they are for.

## The case against usefulness

Every few months someone publishes a method for taking notes. The methods have names and diagrams, and they promise that if you file your thoughts correctly you will one day be able to retrieve them, combined and compounded, as insight. I have tried several. Each one turned my notebook into a second job.

The trouble is that a note written to be found later is written for a stranger: the future self who has forgotten the context and needs everything spelled out. Writing for that person is slow, and worse, it is dull. You stop writing down the half-idea because the half-idea cannot be filed.[^1]

A notebook that nobody will read, including you, has no such problem. It asks only that you finish the sentence.

> How do I know what I think until I see what I say?
>
> — E. M. Forster

### What actually goes in

Mostly things I noticed. A phrase overheard on a bus. The exact colour of a wall. An argument I lost and the reply I thought of two days later. Very occasionally, an idea. I do not date the entries and I do not index them.

### What happens to it

Nothing, as far as I can tell. When a notebook is full I put it on a shelf with the others. I have opened an old one perhaps three times, and each time I was embarrassed within a page.

## Attention is the product

It took me years to see that the notebook was never storage. The writing down is the whole event. To describe the colour of a wall you have to look at the wall, and looking is a thing most of us do far less than we believe.

Consider how often you could draw, from memory, the front of the building you live in. How many windows? Which way does the door open? We move through a world we have mostly agreed not to see, because seeing is expensive and the rent is due.

The notebook is a small, regular tax on that agreement. For the length of one sentence I have to pay attention, and attention, once paid, is not refunded. It leaves a residue. I do not remember the sentence, but I remember the wall.

## A few rules I do not always follow

1. Write by hand. Typing is too fast to notice anything.
2. One sentence is enough. Two is showing off.
3. Never go back and improve an entry.
4. Do not photograph the page.
5. If you are writing it so that someone will admire it, stop, and write down that you wanted to be admired.

## Against the archive

There is a kind of sadness in the modern insistence that everything be kept. We photograph meals so that we will have had them. We record the concert and watch it through the screen. The archive grows, and the afternoon it was meant to preserve passes without anyone in it.

I am not arguing for forgetting. I am arguing that remembering is something a person does, not something a device holds, and that the doing is most of the value. A notebook nobody will read is a way of practising memory without accumulating evidence.

When I die somebody will find forty-odd notebooks and, I hope, throw them away without opening them. They were never the point. The point was the eleven years of looking up.

[^1]: The half-idea is the most valuable kind. A whole idea has usually been had by someone else already.
